This guide teaches digital hardware from the bottom up. It starts with how a voltage on a wire comes to stand for a 0 or a 1, builds through logic gates, arithmetic circuits, memory elements and state machines, and arrives at processors, memory systems, microcontrollers and GPUs. Along the way it covers the electrical layer (circuits, transistors, CMOS, interfacing between chips) and the implementation layer (Verilog, power, heat and the chip design flow). No prior hardware knowledge is assumed, but the explanations are written to be precise rather than casual: every term is defined, every formula is derived or justified, and worked examples show each method step by step.

Highlighted words are the key terms of a section; in solutions the highlight marks the answer. In diagrams and timing diagrams, cardinal marks the path or signal under discussion, gold marks a signal at logic 1 or an active element, and orange marks errors, unknown (X) values and cache misses. Each module's summary is a checklist of what you should now be able to state or do; if an item is unfamiliar, reread its section before starting the problems.

Practice problems

Every module ends with problems at four levels. The level badge carries a small meter so the difficulty is readable at a glance.

Intro

Checks a definition or a single fact. You should be able to answer it right after reading the section.

Easy

Applies one method to a new case: a conversion, a truth table, one formula.

Medium

Combines two or three ideas, or asks you to design something small. Typical of a course exam.

Hard

A multi-step design or analysis, often with a trap. Typical of a technical interview.

Work each problem on paper before opening anything. If you are stuck, open the hint; then check yourself against the solution, which shows the full reasoning rather than just the answer. Tick Got it only when you could solve the problem again without looking. Notation

Boolean algebra has several competing notations. This guide uses the first column; you will meet the others in textbooks, datasheets and code.

Written here | Meaning | Also written A′ | NOT A, the complement of A | A, ¬A, ~A (C bitwise), !A (C logical) A·B or AB | A AND B | A∧B, A & B, A && B A + B | A OR B (not addition, unless the context is arithmetic) | A∨B, A | B, A || B A ⊕ B | A XOR B, exclusive OR | A ^ B 1011₂, 0b1011 | a binary number | 1011b 0x2F, 2F₁₆ | a hexadecimal number | 2Fh, 8'h2F (Verilog) X[7:0] | bits 7 down to 0 of a multi-bit signal X | X7..0 Q* or Q⁺ | the next value of state Q, after the coming clock edge | Q(t+1), Qnext

Units follow SI. Memory sizes use binary prefixes when the distinction matters: 1 KiB = 2¹⁰ bytes, 1 MiB = 2²⁰ bytes, 1 GiB = 2³⁰ bytes. Time and frequency use decimal prefixes: 1 ns = 10⁻⁹ s, 1 GHz = 10⁹ Hz.
